How partner training works
The station system, explained
The station system
1
Split up at the start of each block
One of you takes Station A, the other takes Station B. The two exercises in a block are always opposing movements, so you're never fighting over the same equipment.
2
Work at the same time
While one presses, the other rows. Nobody stands around. Your partner's working set is your rest period — that's the whole trick.
3
Swap after each set
Finish your set, switch stations, go again. Repeat until you've both completed all the listed sets for both exercises.
4
Load independently
You'll be using different weights on most lifts and that's completely fine. The structure is shared; the loads are personal.
Progression rules
1
Hit the top of the range, add weight
If a lift says 8–10 reps and you complete 10 clean reps on every set, add load next session. Small jumps: 5 lb upper body, 10 lb lower body.
2
Leave 1–3 reps in reserve
Stop each set when you could still do 1–3 more good reps. Going to absolute failure on every set adds fatigue without adding much stimulus.
3
Log it or lose it
Write down weight and reps every session. Progressive overload isn't a vibe — it's a number that has to go up over time.
4
Deload every 6–8 weeks
Cut sets roughly in half for one week, keep the weight. You'll come back stronger and your joints will thank you.

Why it's built this way
The Why
Every design choice here traces back to published research. Fair game to fact-check.
The evidence
The reasoning behind the programming
Superset structure
Antagonist supersets cut session time roughly in half without costing you gains
A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found supersets reduce session duration without compromising training volume, muscle activation, perceived recovery, or long-term adaptations in strength, strength endurance, and hypertrophy. Critically, the subgroup analysis showed agonist–antagonist pairings let lifters complete significantly more total reps than traditional straight sets — likely because pre-activating the antagonist enhances neural drive on the following lift. That's exactly why every block here pairs a push with a pull, or a quad movement with a hamstring movement.
Source: Systematic review & meta-analysis, Sports Medicine (2025) — superset vs. traditional prescriptions
Superset selection
Not all supersets are equal — same-muscle pairings actually reduce your volume
The same meta-analysis found "similar biomechanical" supersets (two exercises hitting the same muscle back to back) significantly reduced total volume load compared to straight sets. Agonist–antagonist pairings maintained or increased it. Every pairing in this program is deliberately antagonist or non-competing — you'll never see chest press supersetted with chest fly here.
Source: Sports Medicine (2025) subgroup analysis — SMD −1.08 for biomechanically similar supersets
HIIT placement
Short, hard intervals after lifting protect your muscle gains
The concurrent training literature suggests interference depends heavily on the protocol used. Very intense, short-duration interval work substantially reduces the odds of interference with strength and hypertrophy, while long intervals performed before resistance training with no rest between are the worst-case scenario. A recent 16-week trial using long-interval HIIT found protein synthesis, satellite cell activity, and type II fibre growth all remained intact — though strength gains were still somewhat lower than lifting alone. Every finisher in this app is short, hard, and placed at the end for that reason.
Sources: Sports Medicine interference model (2021); J Appl Physiol concurrent training trial (2025)
Weekly volume
10–20 hard sets per muscle per week is the responsive range
Schoenfeld and colleagues' foundational meta-analysis established a dose-response relationship between weekly set volume and muscle growth, with studies at 10+ weekly sets per muscle showing meaningfully greater hypertrophy than lower-volume protocols. A later systematic review narrowed the practical recommendation to 12–20 weekly sets for trained individuals. Running two upper and two lower sessions per week puts each major muscle group squarely inside that window — without the recovery cost of chasing very high volumes.
Sources: Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger (2017), J Sports Sci; Baz-Valle et al. (2022) systematic review
Training frequency
Twice-weekly frequency per muscle beats once
Splitting weekly volume across two sessions per muscle group rather than cramming it into one lets you keep per-session quality high while accumulating the same total work. That's the entire logic of the Upper / Lower / Upper / Lower layout — chest, back, shoulders, arms, quads, hams, and glutes each get two exposures per week with at least 48 hours between.
Consistent with dose-response meta-regression findings on volume and frequency (Sports Medicine, 2026)
The honest caveat
Supersets cost more than they look like they do
The research is clear that supersets induce higher internal loads, more muscle damage, and higher perceived exertion than traditional sets. That's the trade for the time saving. Practically: expect these sessions to feel harder than the set count suggests, respect the rest days, and don't stack a brutal finisher onto every single session if you're both feeling beaten up. Scale the finisher, or skip it.
Source: Sports Medicine (2025) — noted higher internal load and RPE with superset prescriptions
